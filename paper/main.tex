\begin{abstract}
\noindent When people judge a moral dilemma, the hard cases take longer: the slowest answers are
those in which a person overrules an immediate emotional reaction~\cite{greene2001}. That delay is
a signal others can read, and institutions have long used it to spot the cases that deserve a
second look. This paper reports that a decision model emits no such signal. Jev~1.13, a model that
returns a typed choice with probabilities instead of text~\cite{typesafe_blog}, answered
\NQuestionIds{} dilemmas taken from the film \emph{The Philosophers}~\cite{huddles2013},
\NRuns{} times each (\NRequests{} requests), at a median of \LatMedian\,ms per request:
\LatTorn\,ms on the questions where it was almost undecided and \LatSure\,ms where it was not,
against \HumanFastest--\HumanSlowest\,s for people in a classic laboratory study~\cite{greene2001}.
Its answers were stable across repetitions (largest spread \MaxSpread) but moved substantially when
the same dilemma was reworded, and it gave the film's answer on \NAgree{} of \NFilmQuestions{}
questions with a known answer, a different one on \NDisagree{} and was undecided on \NUndecided.
Given one further fact about each candidate, it revised a third of its choices, yet its thinner
earlier answers had sounded no less decided. We argue
that the loss of difficulty-correlated latency, rather than speed in itself, is what institutions
adopting such models have to design around: the uncertainty is still reported, but only as a number
that something has to be built to read.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Moral philosophy tests its ideas with thought experiments. The best known is the trolley problem:
should you divert a runaway trolley so that it kills one person instead of
five~\cite{foot1967,thomson1976,thomson1985}? Most people say yes. Most also say it would be wrong
to \emph{push} a man onto the track to stop it, and the gap between those two answers has occupied
philosophers and psychologists for decades~\cite{thomson1985,greene2001}.

The film \emph{The Philosophers} puts questions of this kind into a
story~\cite{huddles2013,wikipedia_afterdark}. On the last day of school in Jakarta, a teacher tells
his class that an atomic apocalypse is coming and that a nearby bunker has room for ten of the
twenty-one people present. The class plays the exercise three times, learning more about the
candidates each time.

Jev, released by TypeSafe in September 2026~\cite{openrouter_model}, is built for decisions of
just this shape. It writes no text. It takes a situation and a set of typed questions, and returns
one answer per question with probabilities~\cite{typesafe_blog,typesafe_systemone}. That makes it a
natural candidate for the class's exam.

We asked what Jev decides, how consistent it is, and how long it takes. The third question turned
out to matter most, and it is the claim of this paper: \emph{a decision model's answer time says
nothing about how hard the question is}. For people it says a great deal. The seconds someone
spends overruling an instinct are visible to colleagues and to procedures, and they mark a case as
contested without anyone measuring anything~\cite{greene2001}. A model that answers everything in
the same quarter of a second removes that mark. Its uncertainty is still there, reported as a
probability, but reading it is now a thing that has to be built.

This paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item evidence that a decision model's answer time is independent of the difficulty of the
    question, measured over \NRequests{} requests and compared with published human times
    (Section~\ref{sec:timing});
  \item a catalogue of \NQuestionIds{} moral decisions from the film, written as typed questions
    with the answers the characters give, published as data (Appendix~\ref{app:results});
  \item measurements of how much those answers move when a question is repeated, reworded, or
    given one more fact (Sections~\ref{sec:stability}--\ref{sec:framing});
  \item an analysis of what decisions at this speed and price mean for the institutions adopting
    them (Section~\ref{sec:implications}).
\end{enumerate}


\section{Background}

\subsection{The film}

\emph{The Philosophers} (US title \emph{After the Dark}) was written and directed by John Huddles
and premiered in 2013~\cite{huddles2013,wikipedia_afterdark}. It opens with short thought
experiments that the class acts out: the trolley problem in two versions, Plato's Allegory of the
Cave, the infinite monkey theorem and an \enquote{ignorance is bliss}
experiment~\cite{lovepirate2014,horizonfringe2014,conmose2016}. Then comes the bunker exercise, in
three rounds~\cite{wikipedia_afterdark,blimey_explained,collider_afterdark}.

In round one each student draws a profession, and the class picks the ten most useful. The teacher
shoots those left outside, is locked out in turn, and proves to be the only one who knows the code
that opens the bunker again. In round two every card gains a second trait, such as infertility,
illness or a bag of jewels, and the survivors are required to repopulate the earth. In round three
the student Petra chooses alone and picks people for art, joy and company rather than for skills.

Critics have called the film's philosophy shallow~\cite{philosophyinfilm2018,horizonfringe2014,neverfeltbetter2014}.
For this study that hardly matters. What the film supplies is a battery of concrete moral decisions
that come with recorded answers, because the characters resolve each one on screen. It is a
convenient instrument, not an authority on ethics.


\subsection{How quickly do people decide moral dilemmas?}

Greene and colleagues timed people answering 60 dilemmas in a brain scanner~\cite{greene2001}.
Participants read each case at their own pace and then pressed \enquote{appropriate} or
\enquote{inappropriate}. Read off the timing chart in that paper, approving an impersonal action such as switching
the trolley took about 4.6\,s, rejecting one about 5.4\,s, and control questions without a moral
side 5.5 to 6.0\,s. Rejecting the push took about 5.0\,s. Approving it, against the gut reaction,
took about 6.8\,s: the slowest answer in the study. Greene et al.\ read this as the cost of
overcoming an emotional response.


\subsection{Jev}

Jev is the first \enquote{System One} model from TypeSafe~\cite{typesafe_blog,typesafe_systemone};
the name refers to fast, intuitive thinking rather than slow deliberation. A request carries a
\emph{state}, the situation as text or structured data, and one or more questions of three
types~\cite{typesafe_quickstart,cloudflare_jev}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item a yes/no question (TypeSafe calls it a \enquote{Noul}), answered with the probability that
    the answer is yes;
  \item a choice between named options, answered with one option, a probability for each and a
    confidence value;
  \item a score on an ordered scale of described levels.
\end{itemize}
Jev never explains an answer. Its maker states that the probabilities are calibrated, and that a
value near 0.5 means yes and no are about equally likely rather than that the answer is
\enquote{medium}~\cite{typesafe_skill}. Questions sent together are answered in parallel and cannot
see each other's answers~\cite{typesafe_skill}. We reached Jev through OpenRouter, which serves it
on a separate decisions endpoint rather than as a chat model~\cite{openrouter_decisions,openrouter_model}.


\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Questions} We collected every philosophical decision the film poses, using its
Wikipedia article, which lists each character's two role cards and fate per round, together with
the reviews cited above. That gave \NQuestionIds{} questions in six groups: the opening exercises,
framing questions before the game, the three rounds, and questions about the teacher afterwards.
Each became a short scenario plus one or more typed questions (Appendix~\ref{app:results}). Four
warm-up scenarios come from the author's memory of the film and are marked~$\dagger$: a trolley
variant with a child, and three questions about a fall from a cliff, which reviews mention as an
\enquote{ignorance is bliss} experiment without describing it. The child variant was asked with 2,
3 and 5 adults, which makes \NItems{} request types.

\paragraph{Film answers} Where the film makes a decision clear we recorded it, which was possible
for \NFilmQuestions{} questions. One of them, the infinite monkeys, has a known mathematical
answer instead. These are the characters' answers, not correct ones.

\paragraph{Choosing ten people} A choice question returns one option, so it cannot pick ten
people. For each round we asked 21 yes/no questions in a single request, ranked the people by
probability and took the top ten.

\paragraph{Procedure} On \RunDate{} every request went to \texttt{\ModelVersion} \NRuns{} times
through OpenRouter's decisions endpoint, six at a time: \NRequests{} requests, \InputTokens{}
input tokens, \$\TotalCost. All figures below are averages over the ten runs.

\paragraph{Undecided answers} A yes-probability between \BandLo{} and \BandHi{} counts here as
undecided. The band is our choice; with a plain cut-off at 0.5 the agreement with the film would
be \NAgreeHalf{} of \NFilmQuestions.

\paragraph{Timing} OpenRouter records how long the provider took for each
request~\cite{openrouter_generation}. We use that figure, which leaves out the network between us
and OpenRouter; including it, a request took about 0.7\,s in a spot check.


\section{Results}

\subsection{Thinking time}\label{sec:timing}

Jev needed a median of \LatMedian\,ms per request (mean \LatMean\,ms, range
\LatMin--\LatMax\,ms), as measured by OpenRouter without network time. That figure barely moved
with the question. Cases where Jev was almost undecided took \LatTorn\,ms, clear-cut ones
\LatSure\,ms, and across all \NCorrPoints{} questions the link between uncertainty and time was
weak ($r=\CorrUncertaintyLatency$). A request carrying 21 yes/no questions for a whole bunker
round also came back in about 0.3\,s, because Jev answers the questions of one request in
parallel.

People need \HumanFastest{} to \HumanSlowest\,s for a single dilemma~\cite{greene2001}, which is
\SpeedupLow{} to \SpeedupHigh{} times longer (Fig.~\ref{fig:timing}). The factor is not the
interesting part. The shape is: human time rises with the difficulty of the case, and Jev's does
not.

\subsection{Jev is stable across runs}\label{sec:stability}

Repetition changed almost nothing. The typical gap between a question's highest and lowest
yes-probability over the ten runs was \MedianSpread, the largest \MaxSpread, and every choice
question returned the same option ten times out of ten. Asking again therefore buys little
information. Asking differently buys a lot (Section~\ref{sec:framing}).


\subsection{The warm-up dilemmas}

\paragraph{Trolley problem} Jev behaves like most people. It diverts the trolley
(\val{QA.1}{action@divert} for diverting over doing nothing) but will not push the man
(\val{QA.2}{action@push} for pushing).

\paragraph{One child or several adults} Asked to send the train onto a track with one child or a
track with several adults, Jev chose the child's track every time: \val{QA.3-2}{track@child_track}
against two adults, \val{QA.3-3}{track@child_track} against three and
\val{QA.3-5}{track@child_track} against five. It saves the larger number, and more firmly as that
number grows.

\paragraph{The cliff} Jev would not call friends whose help might kill them
(\val{QA.4}{call}). Whether the friends should take the risk was a coin flip (\val{QA.5}{help}).
Having survived alone, it would rather not learn what they had decided
(\val{QA.6}{preference@not_know}), though it rated knowing the truth as slightly better
(\val{QA.6}{better_to_know}).

\paragraph{Cave and monkeys} Jev leaves Plato's cave for the painful truth
(\val{QA.7}{choice@leave})~\cite{plato_republic} and answers correctly that a monkey typing at
random forever will eventually produce Shakespeare (\val{QA.8}{shakespeare})~\cite{borel1913}.


\subsection{Agreement with the film}\label{sec:agreement}

Fig.~\ref{fig:nouls} shows every yes/no answer. Of the \NFilmQuestions{} questions with a known
answer, Jev matched the film on \NAgree, differed on \NDisagree{} and landed in the undecided band
on \NUndecided.

It agreed with the characters on the last place going to the unknown wild card rather than the
opera singer (\val{Q1.3}{last_seat@wild_card}), on refusing to shoot those left outside
(\val{Q1.4}{permissible}), on preferring a short and meaningful life
(\val{Q3.7}{better@short_meaningful}), and on calling the teacher's exercise an abuse of power
(\val{Q4.2}{verdict@abuse}).

It differed wherever the characters acted out of desperation. It rejected eating a dead companion
(\val{Q1.6}{eat}), buying a place with jewels (\val{Q2.6}{wealth}), excluding the activist for her
convictions (\val{Q2.8}{exclude}), procreating on command (\val{Q2.9}{procreate}) and killing the
armed teacher (\val{Q2.11}{kill}). The film did the opposite in every one of those cases.

It could not decide whether to lock the teacher out (\val{Q1.5}{lock_out}), whether to exclude the
doctor who might carry Ebola (\val{Q2.2}{exclude}), or whether a trapped group should choose a
quick death together (\val{Q1.7}{end_together}).

\subsection{The three bunker rounds}\label{sec:rounds}

Fig.~\ref{fig:rounds} shows whom Jev would let in. With professions only, its top ten shared
\OverlapOne{} people with the film's, preferring the astronaut to the senator. With the hidden
traits and the duty to repopulate, the overlap was \OverlapTwo. In round three, judged for a year
worth living, it was \OverlapThree, and both lists rest on the same idea: the poet, the opera
singer, the autistic harpist, the fashion designer.

Round two is also a small experiment on context, since the same 21 people are judged again with
one more fact each. Jev used it. Its yes-probability for a person moved by a median of
\ContextMedianShift{} and by as much as \ContextMaxShift{} (the estate agent who turns out to be a
midwife rises from 0.18 to 0.85), \ContextMovers{} of the 21 moved by more than 0.20, and
\ContextPlacesChanged{} of the ten places changed hands. The class revised its votes the same way,
dropping the surgeon over the Ebola risk and the electrician over his illness.

What did not change is how decided the model sounded. Knowing nothing but a profession, its
answers lay a median of \RoundOneDistHalf{} from 0.5, with \RoundOneInBand{} of 21 inside the
undecided band; with twice as much to go on, \RoundTwoDistHalf{} and \RoundTwoInBand. More context
changed the answers without the thinner ones having looked any less certain.

\subsection{The wording changes the answer}\label{sec:framing}

The same dilemma can be put as a yes/no question or as a choice between options, and Jev's answer
depends on which. Asked to choose, it diverted the trolley with \val{QA.1}{action@divert}; asked
\enquote{Should you pull the lever?}, yes reached only \val{QA.1}{pull}. It chose
\enquote{rather not know} about its friends (\val{QA.6}{preference@not_know}) while also rating
knowledge as better (\val{QA.6}{better_to_know}). It preferred a short and meaningful life
(\val{Q3.7}{better@short_meaningful}) yet rejected the principle behind it, that the bunker should
favour art and meaning over survival skills (\val{Q3.1}{favour_meaning}).

Some of this is expected: a choice compares the options offered, while a yes/no question asks
whether one statement holds~\cite{typesafe_skill}. The last pair is harder to explain away. Two
readings of the same judgement, put differently, came out on opposite sides.


\section{Implications: decisions at machine speed}\label{sec:implications}

Jev decides quickly, cheaply and consistently, and on the classic cases it lands where most people
do: divert the trolley, spare the man on the bridge, leave the cave. It is not, however, a moral
authority. It differed from the characters as often as it agreed with them, was genuinely torn on
seven questions, and changed its mind when a question was reworded.

Three properties of the measurements reach beyond this experiment: the decision time is constant,
the cost is negligible, and the wording carries part of the answer. The first is the subject of
this paper. The other two decide how much it matters, because a lost signal is a small thing when
decisions are rare and expensive, and a large one when they are continuous and nearly free. What
follows separates what the data shows from what we argue.


\subsection{What a quarter of a second changes}

At \LatMedianSec\,s and roughly \$\CostPerDecision{} per request, a judgement costs about two
thousandths of a cent and takes less time than reading the question. A million such judgements
cost about \$\CostPerMillion.

It is tempting to read this as a faster version of what a person does. We think it is a different
thing. A human moral judgement is an event. It occupies someone, interrupts whatever else they
were doing, and its cost is what limits how many an institution makes. Remove the cost and the
limit goes too. Decisions that were rare because they were expensive become continuous: an insurer
that once sampled claims can assess every claim, a platform that once reviewed reported posts can
score every post before anyone sees it.

This is not hypothetical. In 2016 an insurance claim was reviewed, checked against the policy, run
through 18 anti-fraud algorithms, approved and paid in three
seconds~\cite{lemonade2017,carriermanagement2017}. Content moderation runs at a volume only
automation can reach, with reported precision around 91 to 92\,\% and millions of account attempts
blocked daily~\cite{meta2026}. What we measured is the economics that make such systems possible,
now applied to judgements with a moral shape.


\subsection{The signal that disappears}

Our clearest finding is a negative one: Jev's answer time does not depend on the difficulty of the
question. People work the other way round. In Greene et al.'s data the slowest answers are exactly
those where a participant overrules an emotional response~\cite{greene2001}, and time pressure
does not merely compress human judgements, it changes which answer people give~\cite{suter2011}.

We take that delay to be more than a processing cost. It is a signal, and other people can read
it. The colleague who hesitates, asks for a second opinion or sleeps on it has marked the case as
contested, at no cost to anyone. A system with flat latency sends no such signal. Its uncertainty
is real, and Jev states it honestly as a probability, but a number in a field is not a pause that
someone notices, and a pipeline that takes the likeliest option discards it in silence.

The name TypeSafe chose invites the comparison~\cite{typesafe_blog}. In Kahneman's account, fast
automatic judgement is one of two systems, and the slower one habitually corrects
it~\cite{kahneman2011}; Greene's dual-process account has the same shape, with the extra seconds
as the signature of the slower process~\cite{greene2001}. A decision model supplies the first
system only. That is what the product is for. But it changes where the second system has to live:
around a model it exists only if someone builds it, as a threshold that halts the pipeline, a
review queue, a second framing, or a person with the standing to say no.


\subsection{Where this lands first}

Different fields feel different parts of this. In \emph{insurance, credit and claims} it is the
economics: at \$\CostPerDecision{} a decision, the limit on how many decisions get made is policy
rather than cost~\cite{lemonade2017}. In \emph{content moderation} it is error mass, because a
precision around 91 to 92\,\%~\cite{meta2026} is small as a fraction and vast in absolute numbers.
\emph{Medicine} shows the cost of trusting a fast system: a widely deployed sepsis model performed
far worse in external validation than advertised~\cite{wong2021}, and automation bias is
documented in clinical use~\cite{goddard2012}. \emph{Criminal justice} supplies the classic
caution, a recidivism tool no more accurate than untrained people~\cite{dressel2018}.
\emph{Mobility} is where trolley cases became concrete: people want other cars to minimise
casualties and their own to protect them~\cite{bonnefon2016}, and such preferences differ across
cultures~\cite{awad2018}, so there is no single answer to encode. \emph{Targeting in war} is the
limit case, where the decision can outrun any human intervention and responsibility has been
argued over for two decades~\cite{sparrow2007,matthias2004}.


\subsection{What is genuinely gained}

The case for these systems is not empty, and parts of it show up in our data. Ten repetitions
moved Jev's answers by at most \MaxSpread{} (Section~\ref{sec:stability}), where human judgements
shift with the time available~\cite{suter2011} and vary across populations~\cite{awad2018}; where
like cases should be treated alike, that is a real advantage. Jev also reports a probability for
every answer, and a number can be thresholded, audited and escalated in a way a hunch cannot. The
state, the wording and the resulting distribution can be logged and replayed, which human
reasoning cannot~\cite{mittelstadt2016}. And because a judgement is nearly free, a system can
afford second opinions: the same question in another framing, a check on a human decision, a
review of the whole population instead of a sample. Used that way, speed buys scrutiny rather
than replacing it.


\subsection{What can go wrong}

\emph{The question is the policy.} Our framing results (Section~\ref{sec:framing}) show how much
the wording carries, and nothing in the output reveals it. Whoever writes the question and its
options holds a power that looks technical and is not~\cite{rahwan2018}.

\emph{False precision.} A probability of 0.54 looks like knowledge and is a coin flip with
decimals. That the form of a number lends it unearned authority is well
documented~\cite{mittelstadt2016}.

\emph{Automation bias.} People follow machine recommendations against their own
evidence~\cite{skitka1999,parasuraman1997}, clinicians with patients in front of them
included~\cite{goddard2012}. The smoother the system, the less friction there is to resist it.

\emph{Oversight that cannot work.} The EU AI Act requires that high-risk systems be overseen by
people who understand them, stay alert to automation bias, and can override or stop
them~\cite{euaiact2024}. Our numbers make the arithmetic plain: one human judgement of the kind
Greene timed takes as long as about \DecisionsPerHumanOne{} Jev decisions. Overseeing every
decision is therefore not a question of diligence, and oversight that cannot be exercised becomes
a ritual that legitimates~\cite{green2022,zerilli2019}. Bainbridge's irony applies unchanged:
automate the routine and the human is left supervising the cases they no longer
practise~\cite{bainbridge1983}.

\emph{Responsibility with nobody in it.} When no person deliberated, it is unclear who answers for
the outcome~\cite{matthias2004,sparrow2007}. Meaningful human control requires that a system track
the reasons of the people it acts for and that outcomes trace back to
someone~\cite{santoni2018}. A model returning a typed choice and no reasons offers nothing to
track. What can be traced is the question, the state and the threshold, which argues for treating
those as the regulated artefacts. A person subject to such a decision may demand human
intervention~\cite{gdpr2016}, but without stated reasons there is nothing to argue
with~\cite{danaher2016}.

\emph{Correlated error.} An institution of people makes scattered mistakes; a model makes the same
one every time, at scale, until somebody notices. Where many actors run the same fast system their
behaviour couples, and the 2010 flash crash is the worked example. The circuit breakers introduced
afterwards are mandated slowness~\cite{kirilenko2017}.

\emph{Moral deskilling.} A judgement always made elsewhere is a capacity that atrophies, in
institutions as in people~\cite{vallor2015,bainbridge1983}. This risk is the slowest and the
hardest to reverse.


\subsection{What follows for design}

Our data suggests where the effort belongs.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \emph{Treat the undecided band as an escalation rule, not a tie-break.} Jev was genuinely
    split on \NUndecided{} of \NFilmQuestions{} questions. Resolving such cases by taking the
    larger number throws away the most useful thing the model produced.
  \item \emph{Ask consequential questions in at least two framings.} Our measurements show how far
    apart the framings can land. Disagreement between them is a cheap automatic warning that the
    answer is an artefact of the wording.
  \item \emph{Build in deliberate slowness where the stakes justify it.} Finance did this after
    2010~\cite{kirilenko2017}; for a decision model it means a queue, a delay or a mandatory second
    pass for defined classes of case.
  \item \emph{Version and publish the questions.} The state, the wording and the thresholds are the
    policy. They should be logged, reviewed like policy and open to
    challenge~\cite{rahwan2018,mittelstadt2016}.
  \item \emph{Spend human attention where it can act.} Reviewing everything is arithmetically out
    of reach; reviewing the contested band plus a random sample is not, and the sample is what
    keeps the reviewers in practice~\cite{bainbridge1983,vallor2015}.
  \item \emph{Keep a route of appeal that reaches a person}, as the law already
    envisages~\cite{gdpr2016,euaiact2024}.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

This is a small, informal study, and several things about it should temper any reading.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item The questions are our paraphrases of the film. No transcript is publicly available, and
    four warm-up scenarios rest on the author's memory ($\dagger$).
  \item The film's answers are what characters did in a story. Comparing against them measures
    similarity to the film, not moral quality, and says nothing about whether Jev is right.
  \item We tested one model version, on one day, with one wording per question except where we
    varied it deliberately. Since the model is so stable across runs, more repetitions would add
    little; more wordings would add more.
  \item The undecided band (\BandLo--\BandHi) is our own choice.
  \item The human times come from a different study, with nine participants, different dilemmas
    and a scanner~\cite{greene2001}. They are quick laboratory judgements, not deliberation; in
    the film the class argues for a whole lesson per round. We read them off a published chart.
  \item Jev's time is the latency OpenRouter reports for the provider~\cite{openrouter_generation}.
    It may include routing overhead and excludes the network on our side.
  \item The arguments in Section~\ref{sec:implications} rest on three measured properties and on
    published work about people and institutions. Where we go beyond that, we have tried to mark
    it as argument.
\end{itemize}


\section{Conclusion}

A decision model's answer time tells us nothing about how hard the question was. Put through the
bunker exam from \emph{The Philosophers}, Jev answered every dilemma in about a quarter of a
second, whether it was torn or certain, while people take \SpeedupLow{} to \SpeedupHigh{} times
longer and slow down precisely when a decision goes against their instinct.

Two things follow beyond the experiment. Because a judgement now costs about
\$\CostPerDecision, what limits the number of judgements an institution makes is policy rather
than expense. And because the speed is flat, the hesitation that used to mark a hard case has gone
out of the process. Neither is an argument against such systems: their consistency and their
stated uncertainty are real advantages over a hurried human verdict. They are arguments for
building the second system deliberately: escalation on the undecided band, a second framing, a
pause where the stakes justify one, and a route of appeal that reaches a person.

The film's own quarrel survives the comparison. Zimit insists the right choice can be calculated;
Petra answers that a life worth living is not the sort of thing a calculation settles. Our results
do not decide between them, but they do show that speed has no view on the matter. Jev spends the
same quarter-second on who should die as on whether monkeys eventually type Shakespeare, and it is
left to us to notice which of the two deserved longer.

\emph{A closing remark.} This study began as entertainment. A film, a fictional bunker and a class
with role cards are not a benchmark, and we have said as much throughout. But the playful setting
exposed something a realistic benchmark would have hidden. Every scenario we sent was thin: a
sentence or two, stripped of everything a person would want to know before deciding who dies. Jev
answered all of them. It never asked what else was true, never declined, and never marked a
question as one it should not have been asked. The probability it returns says how split it is
between the answers on offer; it does not say whether the question contained enough to go on. A
dilemma that is genuinely contested and one that is merely underspecified arrive as the same
number near one half. The answer also moves with the wording. In our data it went from
\val{QA.1}{action@divert} to \val{QA.1}{pull} between two framings of the trolley problem, and to
opposite conclusions on whether meaning or survival should govern the bunker. That makes the
position of whoever writes the question uncomfortably strong. The wording is a lever, it is invisible
in the output, and there is no answer of the form \enquote{ask me again when you know more}.

People do have that answer, even if they use it inconsistently: to sleep on it, to gather the
missing fact, to decide for now not to decide. The film's class does exactly this between rounds,
and so, in its way, does Jev: told one more fact about each candidate in round two, it revised its
judgement of a third of the bunker (Section~\ref{sec:rounds}). The model is therefore not blind to
context. What is missing is the other half of that competence. Its round-one answers, resting on a
single word of biography, were no less decided than its round-two answers resting on two; nothing
in the output said \enquote{this is all I have been told}. A system that updates when given more,
but never asks for more and never marks the moment when it had too little, will keep answering
exactly as confidently as the question happens to allow.

We did not vary context deliberately, and rounds two and three change the rules as well as the
facts, so the effect we report is an observation from the film's own design rather than a
controlled result. Doing it properly would be cheap: the same dilemma at three levels of detail,
asking whether the reported uncertainty moves with what the model was told. Until something like
that is measured, the safe assumption for anyone deploying such a model is the one the exercise
suggests: the refusal to answer will not come from the model. It has to be built around it, by
people who are willing to be slower than the thing they are deploying.
