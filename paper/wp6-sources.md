# WP6.1 — Sources for the philosophical discussion

Verified 20 September 2026. Every entry below was checked against Crossref or the publisher;
DOIs, volumes, issues and page numbers are confirmed. Strike anything you don't want and I'll
write the section without it.

## A. Fast and slow judgement (connects directly to our latency finding)

| Source | Details | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* | Farrar, Straus and Giroux, New York, 2011 | The two-systems vocabulary Jev's maker borrows for the name "System One". Lets us ask whether the label fits: Jev is fast and automatic, but it has no System Two behind it. |
| Greene et al. 2001 | *Science* 293(5537):2105–2108, doi 10.1126/science.1062872 | Already cited. People take longest when they overrule their gut, which is the contrast at the heart of our timing figure. |
| Suter & Hertwig 2011, "Time and moral judgment" | *Cognition* 119(3):454–458, doi 10.1016/j.cognition.2011.01.018 | **New and important:** for people, *how much time they have changes the answer*, not just how long they take. Speed is not a neutral parameter in moral judgement. |

## B. Who is responsible for a decision nobody deliberated

| Source | Details | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Matthias 2004, "The responsibility gap" | *Ethics and Information Technology* 6(3):175–183, doi 10.1007/s10676-004-3422-1 | The classic statement: when a machine decides, the operator may no longer be in a position to answer for it. |
| Sparrow 2007, "Killer robots" | *Journal of Applied Philosophy* 24(1):62–77, doi 10.1111/j.1468-5930.2007.00346.x | The sharpest version of the gap, in the case where the decision kills. Relevant because the film's exercise is literally about who dies. |
| Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven 2018, "Meaningful human control over autonomous systems" | *Frontiers in Robotics and AI* 5:15, doi 10.3389/frobt.2018.00015 | The constructive answer: control means tracking human reasons and tracing back to a person. Gives us a standard to measure 0.28 s decisions against. |

## C. Oversight in practice

| Source | Details | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Bainbridge 1983, "Ironies of automation" | *Automatica* 19(6):775–779, doi 10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8 | The oldest and still the best argument: automating the easy cases leaves people to supervise exactly the cases they are now out of practice for. |
| Parasuraman & Riley 1997, "Humans and automation: use, misuse, disuse, abuse" | *Human Factors* 39(2):230–253, doi 10.1518/001872097778543886 | The vocabulary of misuse and disuse of automation. |
| Skitka, Mosier & Burdick 1999, "Does automation bias decision-making?" | *Int. J. Human-Computer Studies* 51(5):991–1006, doi 10.1006/ijhc.1999.0252 | Experimental evidence that people follow a machine's recommendation even against their own evidence. |
| Goddard, Roudsari & Wyatt 2012, "Automation bias: a systematic review" | *JAMIA* 19(1):121–127, doi 10.1136/amiajnl-2011-000089 | The same effect in clinical decision support: not a lab curiosity. |
| Green 2022, "The flaws of policies requiring human oversight of government algorithms" | *Computer Law & Security Review* 45:105681, doi 10.1016/j.clsr.2022.105681 | Argues that mandated human oversight often functions as a legitimating ritual rather than real control. Directly relevant to what "oversight" can mean at a quarter of a second. |
| Zerilli et al. 2019, "Algorithmic decision-making and the control problem" | *Minds and Machines* 29(4):555–578, doi 10.1007/s11023-019-09513-7 | Why "a human is in the loop" is not by itself an answer. |

## D. Machine speed as a systemic risk

| Source | Details | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Kirilenko, Kyle, Samadi & Tuzun 2017, "The Flash Crash" | *The Journal of Finance* 72(3):967–998, doi 10.1111/jofi.12498 | The worked historical example of a market where decisions became faster than human intervention, and of the circuit breakers built afterwards as deliberate slowness. |

## E. Algorithmic decisions in society

| Source | Details | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Mittelstadt, Allo, Taddeo, Wachter & Floridi 2016, "The ethics of algorithms: mapping the debate" | *Big Data & Society* 3(2), doi 10.1177/2053951716679679 | The standard map of the problem space: inconclusive evidence, inscrutability, misguided evidence, unfair outcomes, traceability. |
| Danaher 2016, "The threat of algocracy" | *Philosophy & Technology* 29(3):245–268, doi 10.1007/s13347-015-0211-1 | Rule by systems whose reasoning citizens cannot contest. Jev is an extreme case: it *has* no reasoning to contest, only a probability. |
| Rahwan 2018, "Society-in-the-loop" | *Ethics and Information Technology* 20(1):5–14, doi 10.1007/s10676-017-9430-8 | Who gets to write the question, and how a society negotiates the values a system encodes. Our framing findings make this concrete. |
| Awad et al. 2018, "The Moral Machine experiment" | *Nature* 563(7729):59–64, doi 10.1038/s41586-018-0637-6 | The closest relative to our study: millions of human answers to trolley-style dilemmas, and evidence that answers vary by culture. A benchmark our single model cannot represent. |
| Bonnefon, Shariff & Rahwan 2016, "The social dilemma of autonomous vehicles" | *Science* 352(6293):1573–1576, doi 10.1126/science.aaf2654 | People want other people's cars to be utilitarian and their own to protect them. Shows that "the right answer" is not a stable target. |
| Dressel & Farid 2018, "The accuracy, fairness, and limits of predicting recidivism" | *Science Advances* 4(1):eaao5580, doi 10.1126/sciadv.aao5580 | A deployed, consequential decision aid that turned out no better than untrained people. The cautionary counterweight to "the model is consistent, therefore better". |

## F. Regulation

| Source | Details | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (AI Act), Art. 14 | Official Journal, 13 June 2024 | Verified wording. Requires that overseers can understand the system, "remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output" (automation bias), "correctly interpret the … output", "decide … not to use" it or "disregard, override or reverse" it, and interrupt it. Our latency result asks what this demands in practice. |
| Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR), Art. 22 | Official Journal, 27 April 2016 | The right not to be subject to a purely automated decision with legal or similarly significant effects. The existing legal hook for individual cases. |

## G. What machine-speed decisions already look like in the field

| Source | Details | What it contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Lemonade, "Lemonade sets a new world record" (2017) and trade coverage | Company blog and PR Newswire, Jan 2017; also *Carrier Management*, 23 Jan 2017 | A documented insurance claim reviewed, checked against the policy, run through 18 anti-fraud algorithms, approved and paid in **3 seconds**; later reported at 2 seconds in the UK. The clearest existing example of a consequential decision at machine speed. |
| Meta, Community Standards Enforcement / Integrity Reports, H1 2026 | Meta Transparency Center | Content moderation at a scale only automation can reach, with enforcement precision reported around 91–92% and millions of account-creation attempts blocked daily. Shows both the throughput and the error mass. |
| Wong et al. 2021, "External validation of a widely implemented proprietary sepsis prediction model" | *JAMA Internal Medicine* 181(8):1065–1070, doi 10.1001/jamainternmed.2021.2626 | A fast clinical prediction model deployed across hundreds of hospitals that performed substantially worse than advertised. The medical counterpart to the recidivism case. |

## How the section will use them

The argument stays anchored to our three measurements. Speed (Section A, D, G), the
responsibility question that speed creates (B, C, F), and the framing sensitivity that makes
the question-writer powerful (E). Every empirical claim about the world gets a citation; every
step that is an argument rather than a finding is marked as such.
